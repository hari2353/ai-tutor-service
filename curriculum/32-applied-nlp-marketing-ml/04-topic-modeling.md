# Latent Dirichlet Allocation Derived, NMF, BERTopic, Coherence — and When LDA Still Wins

> **Module id:** `T32-topic-modeling` · **Tags:** nlp, critical

## The 30-second version

LDA is a generative story: each document is a mixture of topics drawn from a Dirichlet distribution, each topic is a distribution over words also drawn from a Dirichlet, and every word in the document is generated by first picking a topic from the document's mixture, then picking a word from that topic's distribution. Fitting the model means inverting that story — inferring the topic mixtures and word distributions that most plausibly generated the observed corpus — and because the exact posterior is intractable, production systems use one of two approximations: collapsed Gibbs sampling, which analytically integrates out the topic and word distributions and repeatedly resamples each word's topic assignment from a closed-form conditional, or variational inference, which replaces the true posterior with a simpler factorized distribution and optimizes it to be as close as possible, trading some accuracy for speed and scalability to large corpora. NMF factors the document-term matrix directly into non-negative topic and word matrices with no probabilistic story at all, and BERTopic sidesteps the generative-model framing entirely — it embeds documents with a sentence transformer, clusters them, and extracts representative words per cluster after the fact. Choosing between them is a genuine judgment call, not "LDA is old, BERTopic is new": LDA and NMF are cheap, reproducible, and interpretable at corpus scale, and a topic model still beats asking an LLM whenever you need a stable, auditable topic space you can track consistently over time and millions of documents — something a per-document LLM call was never designed to guarantee.

## Why this gets asked

The JD explicitly names Latent Dirichlet Allocation, which means someone on the interview loop has actually built and defended a production topic model — likely against a stakeholder who saw an LLM demo and asked "why don't we just ask the model what the topics are." They want to see you derive the generative story and the inference mechanics well enough to debug a model that's producing garbage topics (all documents collapsing into one topic, or every topic looking identical), and they want the honest, considered answer for when a 20-year-old probabilistic model is still the right engineering choice against a much flashier alternative.

## Lineage: past → present → future

**What came before.** Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA, Deerwester et al., 1990) applied SVD to a term-document matrix to find latent semantic dimensions, and Probabilistic LSA (pLSA, Hofmann, 1999) reframed this generatively as a mixture model over topics — a real improvement, but pLSA has no generative story for a *new* document (it learns a fixed topic mixture per training document with no prior, so there's no principled way to infer topics for unseen text without retraining) and the number of parameters grows with the number of documents, making it prone to overfitting on anything but a fixed, static corpus. The pain that killed it: pLSA couldn't generalize, and had no coherent probabilistic account of "what would a document I haven't seen yet look like."

**Where it stands now.** LDA (Blei, Ng, Jordan, 2003) fixed pLSA's generalization problem by putting a Dirichlet prior over each document's topic mixture, making the model fully generative and giving it a principled way to handle unseen documents (infer their topic mixture under the same prior used in training). Collapsed Gibbs sampling (Griffiths & Steyvers, 2004) gave LDA a simple, easy-to-implement inference algorithm that remains the reference teaching implementation and is still used in production for moderate-scale corpora where its slower convergence is acceptable. Online variational Bayes (Hoffman, Blei, Bach, 2010) made LDA scale to genuinely large, streaming corpora by processing mini-batches rather than requiring a full pass, and is what ships as the default in `gensim`'s `LdaModel`. In parallel, NMF (Lee & Seung, 1999) offered a non-probabilistic alternative — factor the document-term matrix directly, no generative story, no priors — that's often faster and, on many real corpora, produces topics judged more coherent by human raters, without LDA's sampling variance or convergence tuning. Then BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022) reframed the entire problem as embed-cluster-extract instead of generative modeling: sentence-transformer embeddings capture semantic similarity a bag-of-words model structurally cannot (synonyms, short text, paraphrase), UMAP reduces dimensionality, HDBSCAN clusters with the ability to mark outliers as noise rather than forcing every document into some topic, and class-based TF-IDF extracts representative terms per cluster after the fact. The live disagreement is not which method is "better" in the abstract — published comparisons genuinely disagree by domain, language, and document length, with some studies finding BERTopic's coherence scores meaningfully higher and others finding LDA competitive or better on specific corpora — it's whether you need BERTopic's semantic sensitivity and short-text handling badly enough to pay its cost (embedding every document, tuning UMAP/HDBSCAN hyperparameters, losing LDA's clean per-document topic-mixture probabilities) against LDA/NMF's cheapness, determinism, and decades of production tooling.

**Where it's heading.** High confidence: hybrid pipelines — a classical or embedding-based topic model to discover and track a stable topic space, an LLM call to generate a human-readable label for each discovered topic from its top words — are already the practical default in most 2026 production systems, because it combines the topic model's cheapness and stability with the LLM's strength at turning "word_1, word_2, word_3..." into a sentence a stakeholder can read. Moderate confidence: BERTopic-style embed-cluster-extract approaches keep gaining share for short-text and multilingual corpora where bag-of-words methods structurally struggle, while LDA/NMF keep their seat for long-document, high-volume, cost-sensitive corpora where the embedding step's cost doesn't pay for itself. Speculative: some argue topic modeling as a standalone technique is being subsumed entirely by LLM-based clustering-and-summarization pipelines as inference cost falls — this is not yet true at the throughput and reproducibility bar most production trend-tracking systems need, and the honest interview answer names the specific gap (cost at scale, temporal consistency of the topic space) rather than conceding the point.

## Mental model

```
LDA'S GENERATIVE STORY (reversed to get inference)

for each topic k = 1..K:           draw word-distribution φ_k ~ Dirichlet(β)
for each document d = 1..D:        draw topic-mixture     θ_d ~ Dirichlet(α)
    for each word position n in d:
        draw a topic             z_{d,n} ~ Multinomial(θ_d)
        draw a word            w_{d,n} ~ Multinomial(φ_{z_{d,n}})

           θ_d (doc's topic mix)              φ_k (topic's word mix)
        ┌───────────────────┐              ┌───────────────────┐
        │ 70% "beach travel" │              │ topic "beach":     │
        │ 20% "family"       │              │  beach 0.08        │
        │ 10% "luxury"       │              │  resort 0.06        │
        └───────────────────┘              │  pool   0.05        │
                    │                        │  ocean  0.04        │
                    ▼                        └───────────────────┘
          pick a topic z for each word, then pick the word from that topic's φ

INFERENCE = run this generative process BACKWARD: given only the words w, recover θ and φ.
Exact posterior P(θ,φ,z|w) is intractable (denominator sums over all topic assignments) →
approximate with COLLAPSED GIBBS SAMPLING (integrate out θ,φ; resample z one word at a time)
or VARIATIONAL INFERENCE (approximate the posterior with a simpler factorized distribution,
optimize to be close).
```

The one thing to internalize: the Dirichlet prior isn't decoration, it's the mechanism that makes the topic mixture *sparse and generalizable*. A low `α` says "most documents are dominated by one or two topics, not a smooth blend of all K" — the same shape assumption that makes real documents (a hotel review is mostly about one or two things, not evenly about fifty) match what the model expects to see.

## How it actually works

### The generative story and the Dirichlet prior, precisely

A Dirichlet distribution is the natural prior over a categorical/multinomial distribution's parameters — it's a distribution over probability vectors (vectors that sum to 1), which is exactly what a topic mixture `θ_d` or a topic's word distribution `φ_k` is. The Dirichlet's concentration parameter controls how spread out or peaked the sampled probability vectors tend to be: a **low** concentration parameter (`α < 1`) produces sparse draws — most of the probability mass concentrated on a few components — while a **high** concentration parameter (`α > 1`) produces smoother, more uniform draws. For `θ_d` (document-topic mixture), a low `α` (a common default is `α = 1/K` or a fitted asymmetric prior) encodes the modeling assumption that real documents are about a small number of topics, not an even blend of all `K`. For `φ_k` (topic-word distribution), a low `β` (similarly often `β = 1/K` or a small fixed value like 0.01) encodes that a topic's word distribution is concentrated on a relatively small, distinctive vocabulary rather than spread thinly across the whole corpus vocabulary — this is the mechanism that produces topics with interpretable, non-generic top words rather than every topic converging toward the corpus-wide word frequency distribution.

The full joint distribution the model assumes generated the corpus:

```
P(w, z, θ, φ | α, β) = [ Π_{k=1}^{K} P(φ_k | β) ] * [ Π_{d=1}^{D} P(θ_d | α) * Π_{n=1}^{N_d} P(z_{d,n} | θ_d) * P(w_{d,n} | φ_{z_{d,n}}) ]
```

Fitting LDA means computing (or approximating) the posterior `P(θ, φ, z | w, α, β)` — what topic mixtures, word distributions, and per-word topic assignments are most consistent with the words we actually observed. The exact posterior requires a normalizing constant that sums over every possible configuration of topic assignments across every word in the corpus, which is combinatorially intractable for any real corpus size — this is why LDA needs approximate inference, and knowing *why* it's intractable (not just that it is) is the first thing an interviewer checks.

### Collapsed Gibbs sampling, derived

Griffiths & Steyvers (2004) noticed that because the Dirichlet prior is conjugate to the multinomial/categorical likelihood, `θ_d` and `φ_k` can be **analytically integrated out** of the model, leaving only the discrete per-word topic assignments `z` to infer — this is the "collapsing" in collapsed Gibbs sampling, and it matters because sampling in the much lower-dimensional, discrete `z`-only space converges faster and more reliably than sampling in the full continuous `(θ, φ, z)` space. The algorithm initializes every word's topic assignment randomly, then repeatedly resamples each word's assignment conditioned on every other word's current assignment, using a closed-form conditional distribution:

```
P(z_{d,n} = k | z_{-(d,n)}, w) ∝ (n_{d,k}^{-(d,n)} + α_k) * (n_{k,w_{d,n}}^{-(d,n)} + β_{w_{d,n}}) / (n_k^{-(d,n)} + Σ_v β_v)
```

Reading this term by term: `n_{d,k}^{-(d,n)}` is how many other words in document `d` are currently assigned to topic `k` (excluding the word being resampled) — this term says "prefer topics this document is already leaning toward," directly implementing the document-topic sparsity the Dirichlet prior encodes. `n_{k,w_{d,n}}^{-(d,n)}` is how many times this specific word type is currently assigned to topic `k` anywhere in the corpus (excluding this instance) — this term says "prefer topics this word type is already strongly associated with." The denominator normalizes by how popular topic `k` is overall. This is a genuinely elegant result: each word gets reassigned by weighing "what does this document need" against "what does this word usually mean," with the `α` and `β` priors acting as smoothing pseudo-counts that prevent a topic or document from collapsing to zero probability early in sampling. Practically, this requires a burn-in period (commonly hundreds to a couple thousand iterations over the full corpus) before the samples are considered to reflect the true posterior, and production implementations typically average statistics over multiple post-burn-in samples rather than taking a single sample as the final answer, since Gibbs sampling produces a Markov chain, not a single deterministic estimate.

### Variational inference, derived

The original LDA paper (Blei, Ng, Jordan, 2003) introduced mean-field variational inference as the alternative: instead of sampling, approximate the true, intractable posterior `P(θ, z | w, α, β)` with a simpler, fully factorized variational distribution:

```
q(θ, z | γ, φ) = q(θ | γ) * Π_{n=1}^{N} q(z_n | φ_n)
```

where `γ` is a per-document Dirichlet parameter and `φ_n` (unfortunately overloaded notation with the topic-word matrix in some texts — here it means the per-word variational topic-assignment distribution) is a per-word categorical parameter. This factorized form assumes `θ` and each `z_n` are independent given the variational parameters — an approximation that's not exactly true in the real posterior, where they're coupled, but tractable to optimize. Fitting proceeds by coordinate ascent on the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**, a quantity that lower-bounds the true log-likelihood of the data and whose gap to the true log-likelihood equals the KL divergence between the variational approximation and the true posterior — maximizing the ELBO is mathematically equivalent to minimizing that KL divergence, without needing to compute the intractable true posterior directly. Concretely, the algorithm alternates: update each document's `γ_d` and each word's `φ_{d,n}` (a per-document, per-word inner loop) until local convergence, then update the global topic-word parameters (an outer loop over the whole corpus). **Online variational Bayes** (Hoffman et al., 2010) modifies this to process mini-batches of documents and take stochastic gradient-style steps on the global parameters, which is what makes LDA scale to corpora too large to hold in memory or too costly to pass over repeatedly — this is the algorithm behind `gensim`'s default `LdaModel`.

**The practical tradeoff, stated plainly:** collapsed Gibbs sampling is simpler to implement and reason about, and given enough iterations converges to samples from the true posterior (it's asymptotically exact); variational inference is biased (the factorized approximation is never exactly the true posterior, no matter how long you run it) but is typically faster and — critically, via the online variant — scales to corpora Gibbs sampling can't practically handle. Production systems fitting LDA on genuinely large corpora (millions of documents) default to online variational inference; small-to-moderate research or exploratory corpora where interpretability of the inference process matters more than raw scale often still use collapsed Gibbs sampling, and it remains the standard teaching derivation because the math is more transparent.

### Choosing K

There is no ground truth for the "right" number of topics, and the honest starting position in any interview answer is naming that directly. Three real approaches, each with a real weakness: **grid search over coherence** — fit models across a range of `K` (commonly something like 5 to 50 in steps of 5) and pick the `K` that maximizes a coherence metric (below) on a held-out or the training corpus; this is the most common production approach but coherence-optimal `K` and human-interpretability-optimal `K` don't always agree. **Perplexity on held-out data** — a classic likelihood-based model-selection criterion, but a well-known and frequently-cited finding (Chang et al., 2009, "Reading Tea Leaves") showed that perplexity is **negatively correlated** with human judgments of topic interpretability in some regimes — a model that fits the held-out likelihood better can produce topics humans rate as *less* coherent, which is why perplexity alone is not trusted as the deciding metric in production topic modeling despite being the most naturally "principled" statistical choice. **Nonparametric alternatives** (the Hierarchical Dirichlet Process, HDP) let the model infer the number of topics from the data itself rather than fixing `K` in advance, trading a hyperparameter for a more complex, slower-to-fit model — a legitimate choice when you genuinely have no prior sense of the right topic count, less commonly used in production because the added complexity often isn't worth it once a reasonable `K` range has been narrowed by domain knowledge.

### Coherence metrics: C_v and UMass

Both metrics score a topic by how well its top-N words (commonly the top 10) tend to co-occur, on the theory that a genuinely coherent topic's top words should appear together in real text more often than chance. **UMass coherence** (Mimno et al., 2011) is intrinsic — it's computed entirely from the training corpus's own document co-occurrence statistics, using a log-conditional-probability formula over ordered word pairs with a smoothing term to avoid `log(0)`:

```
C_UMass = Σ_{i<j} log( (D(w_i, w_j) + ε) / D(w_i) )
```

where `D(w_i, w_j)` counts documents containing both words and `D(w_i)` counts documents containing `w_i`. UMass scores are typically negative, with values closer to zero indicating better coherence; it's fast to compute (no external corpus or sliding-window needed) but because it's computed on the same corpus the model was trained on, it can reward topics that fit the training corpus's idiosyncrasies rather than genuine, generalizable coherence. **C_v coherence** (Röder, Both, Hinneburg, 2015) is more elaborate: it computes normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI) between word pairs using a sliding window over a (often external, larger) reference corpus, then measures indirect cosine similarity between context vectors derived from those NPMI scores. In the systematic comparison that introduced it, C_v correlated most strongly with human judgments of topic quality among the coherence measures tested, which is why it's become the default reported metric in most published topic-modeling comparisons — a C_v score in roughly the 0.4-0.6 range is commonly treated as a rough floor for "acceptably coherent" topics in practice, though this is a soft, corpus-dependent heuristic, not a hard threshold. The real cost: C_v is meaningfully more expensive to compute than UMass, since it needs sliding-window co-occurrence statistics, often over an external reference corpus, for every word pair being scored.

### NMF

Non-negative Matrix Factorization has no generative story at all — it's direct linear algebra. Given a non-negative document-term matrix `X` (rows are documents, columns are vocabulary terms, values are typically TF-IDF weights), NMF factors it into two non-negative matrices `W` (document-topic) and `H` (topic-term) such that `X ≈ WH`, minimizing a reconstruction loss (commonly the Frobenius norm `||X - WH||²` or a KL-divergence variant) via multiplicative update rules (Lee & Seung, 1999, 2001) that guarantee the loss never increases at each step and both factors stay non-negative throughout. The non-negativity constraint is what makes the factors interpretable as "topics" at all — unconstrained matrix factorization (plain SVD/LSA) produces components that can be negative, which has no clean interpretation as "how much of this topic is in this document." NMF is deterministic given a fixed initialization and typically faster to fit than LDA at comparable scale, and on many real corpora — several published comparisons on news and short-text corpora find this — produces topics humans and automated coherence metrics rate as comparable to or better than LDA's, without LDA's sampling variance or the need to tune `α`/`β`. What it gives up: there's no principled probabilistic account of a new, unseen document's topic mixture (no clean generative "prior" to condition on the way LDA's Dirichlet prior provides), and the topic-document weights in `W` are not true probabilities unless explicitly renormalized after the fact — treat them as relative topic strength, not a calibrated distribution, unless you've done that normalization deliberately.

### BERTopic

BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022) is architecturally a different family entirely: **embed, reduce, cluster, extract**, not generate-and-infer. Each document is embedded with a sentence-transformer (producing a contextual, semantic representation that captures synonymy and paraphrase a bag-of-words model structurally cannot — the direct payoff of the contextual-embeddings shift from the previous module, applied here). UMAP reduces the embedding dimensionality (commonly to 5-10 dimensions) because density-based clustering algorithms degrade in very high-dimensional spaces. HDBSCAN then clusters the reduced embeddings — critically, HDBSCAN is density-based and allows documents to be labeled as **noise** (not forced into any cluster) rather than every document being assigned some topic mixture the way LDA's generative story requires; a `min_cluster_size` parameter (commonly starting around 10-15 and tuned per corpus) controls how small a genuine topic cluster is allowed to be. Finally, **class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF)** extracts representative words per cluster: treat every document in a cluster as one concatenated "super-document," compute TF-IDF scores treating clusters (not individual documents) as the unit of comparison, and the top-scoring terms per cluster become that topic's representative words. This handles short text (tweets, reviews, chat messages) and cross-lingual/paraphrase similarity far better than bag-of-words LDA/NMF, because the semantic similarity driving clustering comes from a pretrained embedding model's world knowledge, not corpus-local co-occurrence statistics — the direct cost is that every document needs an embedding forward pass (real compute cost at scale, unlike LDA/NMF's pure count-based fitting), and hyperparameter tuning (UMAP's `n_neighbors`/`n_components`, HDBSCAN's `min_cluster_size`) has a real, sometimes large effect on results with less transparent theory behind the right settings than LDA's Dirichlet-parameter tuning.

### When a topic model still beats asking an LLM

This is the question the module is built around, and the honest answer names specific, measurable reasons rather than defending topic models on principle:

- **Cost and throughput at scale.** Fitting LDA/NMF/BERTopic once on a corpus of millions of documents is a batch job measured in an hour-to-a-few-hours range on modest infrastructure; asking an LLM to classify or summarize millions of documents individually is an API cost and latency bill that dwarfs it, even before considering that a topic model produces a reusable model artifact you apply to new documents cheaply, where an LLM call is paid again per document every time.
- **Temporal and cross-document consistency.** A fixed topic model gives every document a score against the *same* topic space, which is exactly what's needed to track how topic prevalence shifts over months or years — "topic 7's share of content grew from 4% to 11% this quarter" is a well-defined, auditable statement against a fixed topic model. Asking an LLM "what's this document about" independently, repeatedly, across a year of content has no guarantee of using consistent categories call to call, unless you explicitly constrain it to a fixed taxonomy — at which point you've reinvented a fixed topic space and are just using the LLM as an expensive classifier against it.
- **Reproducibility and auditability.** A fitted topic model's parameters (`φ`, the word-topic distributions; `θ`, per-document mixtures) are fixed artifacts you can version, diff between model updates, and inspect deterministically. LLM outputs for the same input can vary run to run (even at temperature 0, across model versions and provider-side updates), which is a real problem for any compliance-adjacent or trend-reporting use case that needs a stable, explainable answer to "why was this document categorized this way."
- **Unsupervised discovery at corpus scale.** A topic model surfaces latent structure across an entire corpus without a human first deciding what the categories should be — genuinely useful for exploratory analysis on a new content domain. An LLM can be extremely good at summarizing a *sample*, but summarizing a representative sample of a multi-million-document corpus and trusting it captured every meaningful minority theme is a different, weaker claim than what an unsupervised clustering pass over the full corpus provides.

Where the LLM clearly wins, honestly: **making topics human-readable.** LDA and NMF output ranked word lists ("beach, resort, pool, ocean, vacation") that a human still has to interpret and label; BERTopic's c-TF-IDF output has the same property. The dominant modern pattern is a hybrid — fit the topic model for the reasons above, then make one LLM call per discovered topic (not per document) to turn its top words into a readable label ("Beach and Resort Travel") — this captures the topic model's cost, consistency, and scale advantages while using the LLM for exactly the part it's genuinely better at and where the call volume (tens of topics, not millions of documents) makes the cost trivial.

## Build it from scratch

```python
# untested sketch — minimal collapsed Gibbs sampler for LDA, enough to see the mechanism;
# gensim's LdaModel (online variational Bayes) is the reference production implementation
import numpy as np

def collapsed_gibbs_lda(docs: list[list[int]], V: int, K: int, alpha: float, beta: float,
                          n_iter: int = 1000, seed: int = 0):
    """docs: list of documents, each a list of word IDs (0..V-1). Returns (phi, theta)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    D = len(docs)

    # random initial topic assignment for every word occurrence
    z = [[rng.integers(K) for _ in doc] for doc in docs]

    n_dk = np.zeros((D, K))           # doc-topic counts
    n_kv = np.zeros((K, V))           # topic-word counts
    n_k = np.zeros(K)                 # total words per topic

    for d, doc in enumerate(docs):
        for n, w in enumerate(doc):
            k = z[d][n]
            n_dk[d, k] += 1
            n_kv[k, w] += 1
            n_k[k] += 1

    for it in range(n_iter):
        for d, doc in enumerate(docs):
            for n, w in enumerate(doc):
                k_old = z[d][n]
                # remove this word's current assignment before resampling ("-i" in the formula)
                n_dk[d, k_old] -= 1
                n_kv[k_old, w] -= 1
                n_k[k_old] -= 1

                # closed-form conditional: P(z=k | rest) ∝ (n_dk+alpha) * (n_kv+beta)/(n_k+V*beta)
                probs = (n_dk[d] + alpha) * (n_kv[:, w] + beta) / (n_k + V * beta)
                probs /= probs.sum()
                k_new = rng.choice(K, p=probs)

                z[d][n] = k_new
                n_dk[d, k_new] += 1
                n_kv[k_new, w] += 1
                n_k[k_new] += 1

    # after burn-in, read off theta and phi from the (smoothed) counts
    theta = (n_dk + alpha) / (n_dk.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) + K * alpha)
    phi = (n_kv + beta) / (n_k[:, None] + V * beta)
    return theta, phi
```

The three things a real implementation adds that this sketch skips: burn-in discarding (don't trust the samples from the first several hundred iterations, since the chain hasn't converged yet), averaging statistics over multiple post-burn-in samples rather than reading off a single sample, and sparse data structures (a real vocabulary and document set makes the dense `n_kv` matrix and per-word Python loop prohibitively slow — production Gibbs samplers use sparse counts and often a compiled inner loop). Read `gensim.models.LdaModel`'s online variational Bayes implementation next for the production-scale approach.

## How it's done in production

**gensim** — the reference Python implementation for LDA (online variational Bayes by default) and its coherence-scoring utilities (`CoherenceModel`, supporting both `c_v` and `u_mass`); the standard first stop for classical topic modeling in Python. **scikit-learn** — `LatentDirichletAllocation` (variational, batch or online) and `NMF`, convenient when topic modeling is one step in a broader scikit-learn pipeline alongside TF-IDF vectorization. **MALLET** (Java) — a widely-used, particularly well-regarded Gibbs-sampling LDA implementation, still cited as a strong-quality reference implementation in comparative studies, sometimes wrapped from Python via `gensim`'s MALLET bridge. **BERTopic** (Python, built on `sentence-transformers`, UMAP, HDBSCAN) — the reference implementation for the embed-cluster-extract approach, with built-in topic reduction/merging, dynamic (time-series) topic modeling, and hierarchical topic visualization. **HDP** (in `gensim`) — the standard nonparametric alternative when `K` genuinely isn't known in advance.

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Every topic looks nearly identical, dominated by the same generic high-frequency words | `β` too high (topic-word Dirichlet prior too smooth), or stopwords/high-document-frequency terms not filtered from the vocabulary before fitting | Lower `β`; apply `min_df`/`max_df` vocabulary filtering (module 1's TF-IDF cutoffs) before fitting, since LDA has no built-in IDF-style downweighting |
| One or two topics absorb the vast majority of documents, the rest are nearly empty | `α` too high (document-topic Dirichlet prior too smooth, or too few topics `K` for the corpus's real diversity) | Lower `α`; re-run the `K` selection sweep with coherence scoring across a wider range |
| Coherence score looks good but topics are subjectively nonsensical to a human reviewer | Coherence metric optimized on the wrong corpus (UMass on a narrow, idiosyncratic training corpus) or coherence-optimal `K` diverging from interpretability-optimal `K` | Cross-check with C_v on an external reference corpus if possible, and always have a human sanity-check the top-N words per topic before trusting the coherence number alone |
| Gibbs sampler results vary meaningfully between runs with different random seeds | Insufficient burn-in iterations, or too few post-burn-in samples averaged | Increase burn-in (commonly hundreds to a couple thousand iterations); average over multiple post-burn-in samples rather than reading off one |
| BERTopic marks a large fraction of documents as noise (-1 topic) | `min_cluster_size` set too high for the corpus's natural cluster sizes, or the embedding model poorly suited to the domain/language | Lower `min_cluster_size`; verify the embedding model was trained on comparable domain/language text; consider `HDBSCAN`'s soft-clustering option to assign probabilistic membership instead of hard noise labels |
| Topic model trained on last quarter's data no longer fits well on this quarter's content | Vocabulary and topic drift — new terminology, new campaigns, seasonal content shifts | Refit periodically on a rolling window rather than treating the model as a permanent artifact; track coherence over time as a drift signal, similar to the OOV-rate monitoring pattern from the classical NLP module |

## Tradeoffs & when NOT to use it

- **Don't use LDA (or NMF, or classical bag-of-words methods generally) on short text (tweets, single-sentence reviews, chat messages) without real caution.** Bag-of-words co-occurrence statistics are sparse and noisy at short document lengths, and LDA in particular tends to produce poor, unstable topics on very short documents — BERTopic's semantic embeddings handle this regime meaningfully better because similarity comes from pretrained world knowledge, not corpus-local co-occurrence.
- **Don't trust perplexity as the deciding metric for choosing K or comparing models.** The Chang et al. (2009) finding that perplexity can be negatively correlated with human-judged topic interpretability is not a footnote — it's the reason coherence metrics (C_v, UMass) became the standard instead, and citing perplexity alone as your model-selection criterion is a real gap an interviewer will probe.
- **Don't fit a fresh topic model per reporting period if consistency over time matters.** Refitting from scratch each quarter produces an unrelated topic space each time (topic 7 in Q1 has no relationship to topic 7 in Q2), destroying the temporal-tracking advantage that's one of topic modeling's strongest reasons to exist; either fix the topic space and re-infer document mixtures against it (LDA's principled way of handling new documents under a fixed model), or use dynamic/online topic modeling designed for this (BERTopic's dynamic topic modeling, or online LDA with periodic incremental updates) rather than full refits.
- **Don't use NMF when you need calibrated per-document topic probabilities for downstream probabilistic reasoning.** NMF's `W` matrix gives relative topic strength, not a normalized probability distribution, unless you explicitly renormalize — if a downstream system needs `P(topic|document)` as an actual probability (feeding a Bayesian pipeline, for instance), LDA's generative framing gives you that natively and NMF does not.
- **Don't reach for BERTopic by default without weighing its real cost.** Embedding every document is real compute (unlike LDA/NMF's pure count-based fitting), and UMAP/HDBSCAN hyperparameter sensitivity is genuinely less transparent to reason about than LDA's Dirichlet parameters — it's the right choice for short text, multilingual corpora, or when semantic (not just lexical) similarity matters, and often unnecessary overhead for long, single-language, topically distinct documents where LDA/NMF already produce coherent topics at a fraction of the cost.

## Interview questions

### Q1 — Derive LDA's generative story and explain what the Dirichlet prior is doing.
**Testing:** whether you can actually derive it, not just name it.
**Answer:** For each topic, draw a word distribution `φ_k ~ Dirichlet(β)`; for each document, draw a topic mixture `θ_d ~ Dirichlet(α)`; for each word position, draw a topic `z ~ Multinomial(θ_d)`, then a word `w ~ Multinomial(φ_z)`. The Dirichlet prior's concentration parameter controls sparsity of the sampled probability vector — a low `α` encodes that real documents are dominated by a few topics, not an even blend of all `K`; a low `β` encodes that a topic's word distribution concentrates on a distinctive vocabulary rather than the corpus-wide word frequency baseline.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Why Dirichlet specifically, not some other distribution over probability vectors?"* — Dirichlet is conjugate to the multinomial/categorical distribution, meaning the posterior after observing multinomial data is again Dirichlet, which is exactly what makes collapsed Gibbs sampling's closed-form conditional distribution possible; a non-conjugate prior would remove that closed form and force fully numerical inference.

### Q2 — Why is exact posterior inference in LDA intractable?
**Answer:** The posterior `P(θ,φ,z|w,α,β)` requires a normalizing constant that sums the joint probability over every possible assignment of topics to every word in the corpus — combinatorially many configurations, growing exponentially with corpus size, with no closed form for that sum in general.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Doesn't the Dirichlet-multinomial conjugacy fix this?"* — conjugacy makes each individual conditional distribution tractable (this is exactly what collapsed Gibbs sampling exploits), but it doesn't make the full joint posterior over all latent variables simultaneously tractable — that's why both standard inference methods are iterative approximations (sampling or variational optimization), not a single closed-form computation.

### Q3 — Derive the collapsed Gibbs sampling update rule and explain each term.
**Answer:** `P(z_{d,n}=k | rest) ∝ (n_{d,k}^{-i} + α_k) * (n_{k,w}^{-i} + β_w) / (n_k^{-i} + Σ_v β_v)`. The first factor is how many other words in this document are currently assigned to topic `k` — prefers topics the document already leans toward. The second factor is how often this specific word type is assigned to topic `k` elsewhere in the corpus — prefers topics this word usually means. The denominator normalizes by topic popularity. `θ` and `φ` are analytically integrated out (the "collapsing"), leaving only the discrete `z` assignments to sample.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Why does collapsing improve convergence?"* — sampling in the lower-dimensional, discrete `z`-only space mixes faster than sampling in the full continuous `(θ,φ,z)` space, because integrating out `θ` and `φ` removes a large source of sampling variance that would otherwise slow convergence of the Markov chain.

### Q4 — Compare collapsed Gibbs sampling and variational inference for LDA: what does each trade away?
**Answer:** Gibbs sampling is asymptotically exact — given enough iterations it converges to true samples from the posterior — but is slower per iteration and its convergence is harder to diagnose (you're trusting a Markov chain has mixed, not optimizing a monotonically improving objective). Variational inference optimizes the ELBO via coordinate ascent, which is typically faster and, via the online/mini-batch variant, scales to corpora too large for repeated full passes — but it's biased: the factorized variational distribution is never exactly the true posterior, no matter how long you run it, because the independence assumption between `θ` and `z` doesn't hold in the true posterior.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Which would you use for a corpus of 50 million documents?"* — online variational Bayes, without much hesitation; Gibbs sampling's per-iteration cost and need for many full corpus passes to converge doesn't scale to that volume the way mini-batch variational updates do, and the bias tradeoff is usually acceptable relative to simply being able to fit the model at all.

### Q5 — What's the actual difference between the ELBO and the true log-likelihood, and why does maximizing the ELBO make sense as a proxy?
**Answer:** The gap between the ELBO and the true log-likelihood of the data equals the KL divergence between the variational approximation `q` and the true posterior `P`. Since KL divergence is always non-negative, the ELBO is a genuine lower bound on the true log-likelihood, and maximizing the ELBO with respect to the variational parameters is mathematically equivalent to minimizing that KL divergence — you're making your tractable approximation as close as possible to the intractable true posterior, without ever needing to compute the true posterior directly.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Can the ELBO ever exceed the true log-likelihood?"* — no, by construction (KL divergence is non-negative), and if you observe that numerically in an implementation, that's a bug — a strong, concrete red flag to name if asked to debug variational inference code.

### Q6 — How would you choose K for a new corpus with no prior domain knowledge of how many topics it should have?
**Answer:** Fit models across a range of `K` (a common sweep is roughly 5 to 50 in steps of 5), score each with a coherence metric (C_v preferred over UMass if the added compute is affordable, since C_v correlates better with human judgment), and pick the `K` at or near the coherence peak — then have a human sanity-check the actual top words per topic at that `K`, since coherence-optimal and interpretability-optimal `K` don't always perfectly agree.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Why not just use perplexity on held-out data, since that's the more standard statistical model-selection approach?"* — Chang et al. (2009) showed perplexity can be negatively correlated with human-judged topic interpretability; a model that fits held-out likelihood better can produce topics humans find less coherent, which is specifically why the field moved to coherence metrics as the practical standard despite perplexity being the more textbook-standard likelihood-based criterion.

### Q7 — Explain UMass versus C_v coherence: what does each actually measure, and what's the practical tradeoff?
**Answer:** UMass computes log-conditional co-occurrence probabilities between a topic's top words using only the training corpus's own document co-occurrence statistics — fast, intrinsic, but can reward topics that fit the training corpus's idiosyncrasies rather than generalizable coherence. C_v computes NPMI-based co-occurrence over a sliding window (often against a larger external reference corpus) and measures indirect cosine similarity between the resulting context vectors — more expensive to compute, but correlates most strongly with human judgment among tested coherence measures in the systematic comparison that introduced it.
**Follow-up trap:** *"If C_v correlates better with human judgment, why would anyone still use UMass?"* — cost and speed at scale — UMass needs no external corpus and no sliding-window computation, so for a large `K` sweep across many candidate models, UMass as a fast first-pass filter followed by C_v (or human review) on the shortlisted candidates is a reasonable, commonly used compromise.

### Q8 — What's the core mechanical difference between LDA and NMF, and when would NMF's lack of a generative story actually matter?
**Answer:** LDA is a generative probabilistic model with Dirichlet priors and an inference procedure (sampling or variational) to recover a posterior; NMF is direct matrix factorization of the document-term matrix into non-negative factors via multiplicative updates minimizing a reconstruction loss, with no probabilistic story at all. It matters concretely when you need a principled way to infer topic mixtures for genuinely new, unseen documents under the same prior used at training time (LDA provides this natively) or when you need calibrated topic probabilities for downstream Bayesian reasoning (NMF's `W` matrix is relative topic strength, not a normalized probability, unless explicitly renormalized).
**Follow-up trap:** *"If NMF often produces more coherent topics on real corpora, why use LDA at all?"* — the generative framing's practical benefits (principled new-document inference, calibrated probabilities, well-understood extensions like dynamic/hierarchical topic models built on the same probabilistic foundation) are worth real coherence-score differences in many production settings; the choice depends on whether you need those specific generative-model properties or just want the most human-coherent topics for a static, one-time analysis.

### Q9 — Walk through BERTopic's pipeline and explain why it handles short text better than LDA.
**Answer:** Embed each document with a sentence-transformer (semantic, contextual representation), reduce dimensionality with UMAP (density-based clustering degrades in high dimensions), cluster with HDBSCAN (density-based, allows documents to be marked as noise rather than forced into a topic), then extract representative words per cluster with class-based TF-IDF, treating each cluster as one concatenated document. It handles short text better because clustering similarity comes from the embedding model's pretrained semantic/world knowledge (captures synonymy, paraphrase) rather than from corpus-local word co-occurrence statistics, which are extremely sparse and noisy for documents only a few words long — LDA's whole inference mechanism depends on word co-occurrence patterns that short documents simply don't provide enough of.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Does BERTopic's use of embeddings mean it 'understands' the topics better than LDA?"* — no, it means clustering has access to richer similarity signal upstream; BERTopic's topic *representation* (the words shown to a human) is still just c-TF-IDF over cluster membership, not a deeper semantic understanding of what the cluster means — the interpretive step is identical in kind to LDA's, just fed better-clustered input.

### Q10 — Your LDA model, once fitted, produces two nearly-identical topics with almost the same top words. What's happening and how do you fix it?
**Answer:** Most likely `K` is set higher than the corpus's real topical diversity supports, so the extra topic capacity gets absorbed by splitting what's genuinely one topic into two near-duplicates. Check the coherence-vs-K curve — if coherence plateaus or degrades past a certain `K`, that's the signal you've overshot the corpus's real topic count; re-fit at a lower `K` and check whether the duplicate topics merge back into one coherent one.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Could this also be a sign of insufficient Gibbs sampling iterations rather than K being wrong?"* — yes, that's a real alternative explanation, and the way to distinguish them is checking convergence directly (does the assignment stabilize with more iterations, or does re-running with a different seed and more burn-in produce a materially different, less duplicated topic set) before concluding it's a `K` problem rather than an under-converged sampler.

### Q11 — A stakeholder asks why you didn't just use an LLM to find the topics in a 5-million-document content corpus. What do you say?
**Testing:** the central senior tradeoff of this module.
**Answer:** Cost and throughput first — classifying or summarizing 5 million documents individually with an LLM is a real, recurring API cost and latency bill that a one-time topic-model fit followed by cheap per-document inference doesn't have. Second, consistency — a fixed topic model gives every document a score against the same topic space, which is what trend tracking over time actually requires; independent LLM calls per document have no inherent guarantee of using consistent categories run to run unless constrained to a fixed taxonomy, at which point you've reinvented a fixed topic space anyway. Reproducibility is the third reason — a fitted model's parameters are versionable, auditable artifacts, where LLM outputs can vary across runs and model versions.
**Follow-up trap:** *"So there's no role for the LLM here at all?"* — there's a real, valuable role: use the LLM to turn each discovered topic's top words into a human-readable label, one call per topic (tens of calls) rather than per document (millions of calls) — this is the standard hybrid pattern and naming it shows you're not dismissing the LLM, just scoping it to where its cost is trivial and its strength (natural language generation) is the actual bottleneck.

### Q12 — How would you detect and handle topic drift in a topic model that's been running in production for a year?
**Answer:** Track coherence of the existing fitted model against fresh incoming documents over time — a declining trend suggests the topic space no longer fits the current corpus well, similar in spirit to the vocabulary-drift monitoring pattern from the classical NLP module. Also track the fraction of documents whose best-matching topic has low probability under the current model (a rising "doesn't fit any existing topic well" rate is a direct drift signal). Refit periodically on a rolling recent window rather than treating the original model as permanent, and if temporal comparability matters, be explicit about the tradeoff — a full refit breaks continuity with the old topic space (topic 7 pre-refit isn't topic 7 post-refit), so either use a topic-model variant designed for temporal consistency (dynamic topic modeling) or accept and document the discontinuity.
**Follow-up trap:** *"How often would you refit, concretely?"* — there's no universal number; it should be driven by the observed drift rate (coherence decline, rising doesn't-fit-well rate) against the corpus's actual content velocity — a marketing content platform with frequent new campaigns and seasonal shifts likely needs more frequent refitting than a slower-moving domain, and the honest answer names measuring drift directly rather than picking an arbitrary calendar cadence.

### Q13 — Design a topic-modeling pipeline for tracking hotel review themes over time across a large multilingual review corpus.
**Testing:** synthesis across method choice, multilingual handling, and temporal tracking.
**Answer:** Multilingual and likely short-to-moderate review length pushes toward BERTopic with a multilingual sentence-transformer embedding model, since bag-of-words LDA/NMF would need separate per-language pipelines and struggle on shorter reviews' sparse co-occurrence statistics. For temporal tracking, fix the topic space at initial fit (or use BERTopic's dynamic topic modeling extension) and infer new reviews' topic assignment against the fixed space rather than refitting from scratch each period, preserving comparability. Validate topic quality with C_v coherence per language (coherence computed on a monolingual sliding window, since cross-lingual co-occurrence statistics don't transfer cleanly) and human review of top representative documents per topic, not just top words, given BERTopic topics are defined by cluster membership rather than a clean generative word distribution.
**Follow-up trap:** *"What if review volume in some languages is too low for BERTopic's clustering to find stable topics?"* — this is a real, common failure mode — HDBSCAN needs enough density to form clusters, and low-volume languages will produce noisy or all-noise (-1) clustering; the practical fix is grouping low-volume languages into a shared multilingual embedding space (relying on the sentence-transformer's cross-lingual alignment) rather than fitting per-language models with insufficient data each.

### Q14 — What's the practical effect of setting `α` and `β` too high versus too low in LDA, and how would you diagnose which one is misconfigured from the model's output?
**Answer:** `α` too high produces documents that look like a smooth blend across many topics rather than being dominated by one or two — check by looking at the entropy or effective number of topics per document's `θ_d`; if most documents have near-uniform topic mixtures, `α` is likely too high (or `K` too low for what the prior is trying to spread across). `β` too high produces topics whose top words look generic and overlap heavily with the corpus-wide word frequency distribution rather than being distinctive; check by comparing a topic's top words against simple corpus-wide term frequency — heavy overlap signals `β` too high. Both too low produces the opposite failure: brittle, overly peaked distributions that can look artificially clean but may be overfitting to sampling noise on a small corpus.
**Follow-up trap:** *"Are α and β usually hand-tuned, or learned from data?"* — both are possible; fixed symmetric defaults (`α=β=1/K` is common) are the simplest starting point, but many production implementations (including `gensim`'s) support learning an asymmetric `α` from the data during fitting, which often produces better results than a fixed symmetric prior since real corpora rarely have perfectly uniform topic-frequency patterns across documents.

### Q15 — Your LDA-based content-tagging model, fit six months ago, is showing declining coherence on recent content. Walk through your diagnosis and remediation plan.
**Answer:** First confirm it's genuine drift, not a measurement artifact — recompute coherence on a fresh sample of recent documents using the same metric and corpus-scope conventions as the original evaluation, since a metric computed differently isn't a valid comparison. If confirmed, check whether it's vocabulary drift (new terms, campaigns, or slang not in the original fitted vocabulary — analogous to the classical-NLP OOV-rate monitoring pattern) or genuine topical drift (the actual distribution of themes in the content has shifted, not just the words used to describe existing themes). Vocabulary drift alone might be addressable with a lighter update (extend the vocabulary, keep the topic structure); genuine topical drift needs a real refit, and if temporal comparability against historical reporting matters, that refit needs an explicit plan for reconciling the old and new topic spaces (documented discontinuity, or a dynamic topic modeling approach designed to evolve topics smoothly over time) rather than silently swapping one static model for another.
**Follow-up trap:** *"The business wants zero discontinuity in topic definitions. Is that achievable?"* — not perfectly, and the honest answer says so — any topic model fit on a changing corpus will drift in some sense, and "zero discontinuity" really means "acceptably smooth, well-communicated transitions," achieved via dynamic topic modeling or incremental online updates rather than full periodic refits, with the tradeoff (some loss of fit to genuinely new themes, in exchange for continuity) stated explicitly to the stakeholder rather than promised away.

## Red flags that fail you

- Describing LDA as "a clustering algorithm" rather than a generative probabilistic model with an explicit inference problem.
- Not knowing why exact LDA inference is intractable, or why collapsed Gibbs sampling's closed form exists (Dirichlet-multinomial conjugacy).
- Confusing what `α` and `β` each control (document-topic sparsity vs. topic-word sparsity).
- Citing perplexity as the deciding metric for choosing K without knowing the Chang et al. (2009) finding about its correlation with human judgment.
- Treating BERTopic as strictly superior to LDA/NMF without naming its real costs (embedding compute, hyperparameter sensitivity, loss of clean generative probabilities).
- Not knowing NMF's topic weights aren't calibrated probabilities unless explicitly renormalized.
- Refitting a topic model from scratch each period and treating the resulting topic numbers as comparable over time.
- Dismissing topic modeling entirely in favor of "just ask an LLM" without naming the cost, consistency, and reproducibility reasons that's often wrong at scale.

## Cheat card

```
LDA GENERATIVE STORY   φ_k ~ Dirichlet(β) [topic-word]   θ_d ~ Dirichlet(α) [doc-topic]
  per word: z ~ Multinomial(θ_d), w ~ Multinomial(φ_z)
  low α → docs dominated by few topics.  low β → topics concentrated on distinctive vocab.
  exact posterior intractable: normalizer sums over every topic-assignment configuration.

COLLAPSED GIBBS (Griffiths & Steyvers 2004)   integrate out θ,φ (Dirichlet-multinomial conjugacy)
  P(z=k|rest) ∝ (n_dk^-i + α_k) * (n_kw^-i + β_w) / (n_k^-i + Σβ_v)
  [doc leans toward k] × [word usually means k], normalized by topic popularity
  asymptotically exact, needs burn-in (100s-1000s iters), average post-burn-in samples

VARIATIONAL INFERENCE (Blei/Ng/Jordan 2003)   q(θ,z|γ,φ) factorized approx, maximize ELBO
  ELBO gap to true log-likelihood = KL(q || true posterior). Biased but fast.
  ONLINE VB (Hoffman 2010): mini-batch, scales to huge corpora — gensim LdaModel default.

CHOOSING K   no ground truth. Sweep K (5-50, step 5), pick coherence peak, human-check top words.
  PERPLEXITY: negatively correlated w/ human coherence judgment in some regimes (Chang 2009) —
  don't use alone. HDP: nonparametric, infers K, more complex, rarely default in production.

COHERENCE   UMass: intrinsic, log-cond-prob on TRAINING corpus, fast, can overfit to corpus.
  C_v: NPMI + sliding window (often external corpus) + cosine sim. Best human-judgment correlation.
  ~0.4-0.6 C_v = soft floor for "acceptable" (corpus-dependent heuristic, not a hard rule).

NMF (Lee & Seung 1999)   X ≈ WH, both non-negative, multiplicative updates, min Frobenius/KL loss.
  No generative story. No native new-doc inference. W not a calibrated probability unless renorm'd.
  Often comparable/better coherence than LDA, deterministic, faster, no α/β tuning.

BERTOPIC (Grootendorst 2022)   embed (sentence-transformer) → UMAP reduce → HDBSCAN cluster
  (density-based, allows NOISE label, min_cluster_size ~10-15) → c-TF-IDF extracts top words/cluster
  Handles short text/multilingual/synonymy far better (semantic sim, not corpus co-occurrence).
  Real cost: embedding compute + UMAP/HDBSCAN hyperparameter sensitivity.

WHEN TOPIC MODEL BEATS LLM   cost/throughput at millions-of-docs scale · temporal consistency
  (fixed topic space = comparable trend tracking) · reproducibility/auditability (versioned,
  deterministic artifacts vs run-to-run LLM variance) · unsupervised discovery over full corpus
  (not just a sample). LLM WINS at: turning topic word-lists into human-readable labels —
  standard hybrid: fit topic model, ONE LLM call per topic (not per document) to label it.
```
